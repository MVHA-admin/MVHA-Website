Who’s the Oldest?

In search of Mountain View’s oldest living resident, the Historical Association bestowed the honour on a WWII veteran who credits a long life to survival genes, mixed with a bit of luck.

By Emily Margaretten. Photographs by Anna Hoch-Kenney.

It has become something of a yearly tradition for Suzanne Epstein to modify her father’s birthday banner ahead of the family celebrations. “After 100, it becomes really hard to find banners,” Epstein laughed, showing her handiwork of taping over the last digit to reflect her father’s true age.

On Tuesday 15 October 2024, Lloyd Lettis turned 104 in the Mountain View home he bought for just under $13,000 in 1952. Lettis still jokes that he thinks he paid too much for it.

Lettis also holds the honour of winning the Mountain View Historical Association’s “oldest living resident” competition. The competition received entries from a couple of other centenarians, but Lettis was the oldest, according to Carol Donahue, a Historical Association board member. Still, Lettis is quick to point out that he probably should be considered a runner-up: his good friend Elzene Yancey likely holds the distinction, since she is nine months older than him but did not enter.

Born in 1920, Lettis attributes his long life to a combination of luck and perseverance — something he says he inherited from his parents, who immigrated to the US from Croatia around the turn of the century. “I think we have what they call a survival gene, so we just survived,” Lettis said.

His father and mother met in Watsonville, an agricultural town with a large Croatian population. Both came to the US under fairly modest circumstances but managed to gain a foothold in the apple orchards. Lettis’s father worked his way up from apple packer to landowner, acquiring multiple orchards of his own.

As a young child, Lettis worked hard in the orchards helping his father and brothers. They were comparatively well off; at that time, people looking for work would come to their house asking for jobs in the orchard fields. Even if there was nothing available, his mother would always give the person a cup of coffee.

The family’s fortunes changed a few years into the Great Depression. His father had taken out loans, expecting to pay them back after the apple harvest. But a shipyard strike prevented the apples from reaching market. Creditors called in their loans and the orchards fell like dominos. They were left with just one orchard — a 52-acre plot of land still in the family today.

World War II

Lettis put in long hours in the orchards, but he was also a good student. He attended UC Berkeley with aspirations to become a lawyer. His plans changed when the US entered World War II: Lettis enlisted in the US Army Air Forces in his senior year and was called up for training about a year later.

It was also at Berkeley that Lettis met his wife, Myrtle. He saw her at a college dance and cut in as a partner, only to admit that he didn’t like dancing and ask if she would see a movie instead. Myrtle wanted to see Gone with the Wind, which cost 40 cents a ticket. But Lettis only had 50 cents in his pocket, so they each paid their own way and had nickel Cokes afterwards. “The first date was such a fiasco. I thought I’d never see her again,” Lettis said.

That was not the case, and they married exactly two years later on 2 February 1943. A few days later, Lettis received a telegram ordering him to report to basic training in Florida. The army told him it would provide for all his needs; it did not, however, give him the fare to get to Florida. So, with a bit of luck and a lot of determination, Lettis boarded several trains using his telegram as a ticket while relying on the goodwill of porters who hid him in the bathrooms.

After Florida, the army sent Lettis to Yale and then Harvard to take courses in radar technology. That training eventually led to his career as an engineer — far from his initial aspiration to become a lawyer.

Living in Mountain View

When the war ended, Lettis returned to Watsonville for a few years to work in the apple orchards while raising a young family. He resigned his commission during the Korean War, not wanting to leave his family; as part of the deal, he agreed to take a defence job in the Bay Area.

His family settled in Mountain View in 1952, in a home Lettis negotiated down from $13,000. At the time it was surrounded by apricot orchards, and Lettis quickly distinguished himself as one of the fastest apricot pickers in the neighbourhood, filling two buckets to everyone else’s one.

Lettis and his wife were embraced by the Mountain View community and took an active part in civic life. He was a Boy Scout leader and involved in the Parent Teacher Association as well as local clubs and speaker series, along with his wife.

When his father died in 1968, Lettis took over the apple orchard in Watsonville, commuting long hours and working in the fields to keep it going. His active lifestyle continues: he still tends a vegetable garden in his backyard.

Lettis only recently stopped playing tennis and driving at the age of 102, although his licence is still valid. He plays bridge twice a week and enjoys attending speaker series. He is also remarkably healthy — his medication list is short. He takes one aspirin daily.

Lettis says the hardest part of getting old is outliving his wife and friends. Still, he is surrounded by a big family of four children, 11 grandchildren and 22 great-grandchildren.

Asked how he felt about turning 104, Lettis smiled and added: “I don’t have any feeling about it… I just keep living.”
